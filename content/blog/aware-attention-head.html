Software still lacks a clean answer for what stays the same when a system changes its body. Agent teams lose purpose the first time a context resets; long nesting turns that into a dropped closing date or a repeated week of work. On FractiSI scripted simulators, under a stated six-property profile, an aware attention head was right 94.8% of the time against 68.4% for a strong shared-world baseline and 63.1% for flat voting. Five mission copies plus majority repair held 91.7% survival at 1,024 nesting levels — and copies grow about logarithmically with depth.

This Fractal Goldilocks Channel brief asks a narrow operational question: can a software head hold six shared properties that traditions use when they talk about spirit — continuity through body change, directed attention, new vehicles for new capacities, carrying what matters, scale invariance, and survival of substrate loss — and keep holding them as nesting deepens. Evidence comes from regenerable simulators with ablations, decoy trials, nesting sweeps to 1,024 levels, and substrate-loss sweeps. Agents are scripted, not live. Operational equivalence under the stated profile is not a proof that the profile exhausts spirit in any tradition.

What the simulators measured

Three designs: flat agents that vote; a shared world where every signal is read and votes are weighted by confidence; and a head that checks independent corroboration, builds a specialist shell when a capability gap appears, keeps a persistent mission record, reconstructs only what the next context needs, and holds the mission redundantly with repair at each nesting level.

Across four task families the head is right 94.8 percent of the time, against 68.4 percent for the shared world and 63.1 percent for flat voting, at about the same compute per trial. Removing the evidence check costs 39.5 points, removing meta-attention entirely costs 58, removing shell creation costs 18.5, removing the persistent mission costs 19.5, and removing selective reconstruction costs 9.5. On decoy trials, a head that simply follows any minority is right on none of them; the head that checks corroboration is right on all of them.

At one level of nesting every design does fine. By sixteen levels the single-copy designs are mostly gone, and by sixty-four they are at zero. Five copies with no repair do no better in the long run, because corruption accumulates. Five copies plus majority repair keep 91.7 percent survival at 1,024 levels, nearly flat from the start. Redundancy and repair are each useless alone. Together they hold the mission.

When specialist shells are destroyed 30 percent of the time at every level for 256 levels, a single copy never survives, five copies survive about half the time, and nine copies survive 99.5 percent of the time. At 50 percent destruction even nine copies fall to 58.5 percent. Five copies hold the mission to roughly nine thousand levels before survival drops to even odds; nine copies to about 1.8 million; thirteen to about 350 million. Copy count grows about logarithmically with depth: one copy to depth 16, three to 256, five to 1,024, eleven to a million.

Qualifications matter. Corroboration is a clean flag in the simulator and noisy in real systems. Loss rates are chosen, not measured. Corruption is assumed independent per level; a failure that hits every copy at once would defeat repair. The agents are scripted, not live. The six-property profile is a definition the authors chose; it leaves out experience, value, and meaning because a simulator cannot test them.

Fractal Holographic Goldilocks interpretation: the blocks above are regenerable computational lab receipts on scripted simulators. They support operational equivalence under a six-property profile. They do not prove the profile exhausts spirit, and they do not measure live agents.

Systems. An orchestrating layer that only counts votes adds cost. A layer that checks corroboration, funds specialists, carries the mission, and repairs redundant copies earns its place on long nested jobs — where a dropped detail is a missed closing date.

Buyers. Map property to mechanism before buying another orchestration product. Ablations here show which removals cost tens of points and which are decoration.

Teams. Turning a vague word into a pass/fail checklist lowers the cost of arguing about identity across resets — useful when teams already lose weeks to corrupted hand-offs.

Scale planning. Fidelity at depth is a number: copies grow about logarithmically with nesting. Plan redundancy for the depth you need; do not assume a fixed five-copy design covers infinite octaves forever.
