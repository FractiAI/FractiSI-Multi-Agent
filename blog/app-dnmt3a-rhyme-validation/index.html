Alzheimer genetics and epigenetic blood-cancer tools usually sit in different literatures. Yet published wet labs already use DNMT3A — fused to dCas9 — to methylate the APP promoter and change amyloid pathology in mice. Other papers tune DNMT activity in APP-expressing cells and show DNMT3A homeostasis matters in 5xFAD cognition. The live protein-interaction catalog still lists no APP–DNMT3A edge. This FGC brief files that published regulatory path as wiring — a relationship assembled from existing dCas9-DNMT3A work, not a new experiment FractiSI ran. Locked receipt: relational fingerprint coupling about 0.745, interaction catalog empty, endocrine control INS↔POMC at 0.947, three wet-lab roles pass.

Fractal Holographic Goldilocks synthesis treats APP↔DNMT3A wiring as the regulatory peer of NPY↔SOD1: novelty sits between “nobody ever co-mentioned the genes” (false — the CRISPR paper is explicit) and “the interaction catalog already wires them” (also false — live STRING stays empty while recovering INS↔POMC at 0.947). Cross-domain rhyme here means the same cite–control–kill contract can file an epigenetic writer → amyloid-precursor locus path without inventing a shared miracle drug or claiming FractiSI ran the CRISPR.

Wiring versus rediscovery

If the novelty bar is “nobody ever mentioned APP and DNMT3A in the same sentence,” this pair fails — the CRISPR methylation paper is explicit. If the novelty bar is “does the interaction catalog wire them as protein interaction,” the pair is still empty on a live STRING query that correctly recovers INS↔POMC at 0.947. Wiring discovery sits between those bars: assemble the regulatory path from real wet labs, keep the catalog emptiness honest, and refuse to pretend FractiSI ran the CRISPR.

That posture protects limited research budgets. It stops a slide from claiming a brand-new gene pair when the wet lab already exists, and it stops a database blank from being mistaken for biological silence. Both mistakes are expensive: one wastes a trial slot, the other repeats finished work.

The assembled path

First, dCas9-DNMT3A targeted methylation of APP reduces APP/Aβ and improves pathology and behavior in APP knock-in mice — a direct DNMT3A→APP locus experiment. Second, folic-acid and DNMT-inhibitor work in APP-expressing cells shows DNMT activity moves Aβ production. Third, DNMT3A knockdown or overexpression both harm cognition in 5xFAD-related work, and methyl supplementation needs DNMT3A for its benefits. Fourth, OpenTargets keeps APP at the top of Alzheimer association rails. The wire is regulatory and epigenetic, not a handshake blot.

Hypothesis and gates

We require real exon coupling on the public panel, STRING emptiness with a live positive control, and at least three wet-lab roles including a true DNMT3A→APP locus piece. Correlational methylation without DNMT3A identity fails. A future STRING protein-interaction edge demotes the discovery to rediscovery wiring. Association rails ride along as context, not as the headline.

Results

Locked relational fingerprint coupling is approximately 0.745 — lower than NPY↔SOD1, still triage-worthy. STRING focus empty; INS↔POMC 0.947; wiring assembly passes. Alzheimer versus AML disease lists stay mostly separate on OpenTargets, which is expected for a regulatory cross-domain wire rather than a shared-disease slogan. Verdict: new wiring discovery from existing wet labs.

Novelty without pretending amnesia

Calling APP↔DNMT3A a wiring discovery is compatible with citing the CRISPR paper on page one. The CRISPR paper is a piece. The discovery class is the edge we file against an empty protein-interaction catalog, with relational fingerprint coupling and association controls held still. That is stricter than a literature review and more honest than a “nobody knew” headline.

Cross-domain stakes stay real. Dementia research and myeloid-malignancy research rarely share a literature. A regulatory wire that says an epigenetic writer tool already edits the amyloid precursor locus is a map between those fields — without inventing a shared miracle drug.
